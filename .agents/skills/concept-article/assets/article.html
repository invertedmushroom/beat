Why averaging quiets independent noise

A single reading can wander far from the signal. Averaging gives those errors a chance to cancel. The interesting question is what must be true for that cancellation to keep working.

We will connect a noisy trace, a variance calculation, and a streaming implementation. By the end, you should be able to predict what four times as many readings buys you—and when it buys you nothing.

One signal, many readings

Start with a fixed signal of zero. Each reading adds an error. Our ideal model treats those errors as independent draws from a uniform distribution between −1 and 1, in arbitrary normalized units. Their expected value is zero.

The dashed line below shows one fixed pseudorandom sequence. The solid line shows the running mean of the readings seen so far. Neither line is a measurement from a physical device.

A later reading can move the average farther from zero. The claim is not that every prefix improves. It is that, across independent repetitions of the model, the average becomes less variable.

Independence is doing the work

Call the variance of one reading sigma squared. Variance measures spread across repetitions. For independent errors, the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances. Averaging divides the sum by the sample count n, which divides its variance by n squared.

Var(mean) =nσ2n2 =σ2n

In words: the variance of the mean is the variance of one reading divided by the reading count. Standard deviation is the square root of variance, so it shrinks with the square root of the count rather than the count itself. For our uniform-noise model, the single-reading variance is 1 / 3.

The same mean needs only two numbers

The mathematical definition appears to require keeping every reading. Algebra gives a smaller implementation: retain the count and the current mean, then adjust the mean toward the next reading.

let n = 0;
let mean = 0;

for (const reading of readings) {
  n += 1;
  mean += (reading - mean) / n;
}

For n > 0, mean equals the sum of the first n readings divided by n in exact arithmetic. Floating-point arithmetic introduces rounding. This implementation changes the representation, not the independence assumption or the statistical claim.

More readings do not remove a shared error

If every reading contains the same calibration offset, averaging retains that offset. Correlated errors add covariance terms to the variance of the sum. A changing signal creates another problem: an average of the past may lag behind the present.

The useful synthesis is conditional: independent, zero-mean errors around a fixed signal average out in distribution. The streaming code computes the average efficiently, but it cannot make those assumptions true.

Make a prediction

If the model holds and you collect four times as many readings, what happens to the standard deviation of their mean?

It halves, because 1 / sqrt(4n) = 1 / (2 sqrt(n)). A shared bias does not halve. The result depends on the assumptions, not on how the mean is stored.
